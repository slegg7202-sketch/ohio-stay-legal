The Cleveland Short-Term Rental License Packet

Everything a Cleveland Airbnb or Vrbo host needs to get licensed under the city's new short-term rental law — Ordinance 561-2026 — in plain words, in the right order.

Starting November 28, 2026, renting out a home in Cleveland for stays of 30 days or fewer requires a short-term rental license (Cleveland Codified Ordinances Chapter 686B). The license application asks for 12 specific items, and several of them — a lead-safe certification, a certificate of occupancy, the right insurance — take weeks to get. This packet lists every requirement, explains how to get each one, and shows you the parts hosts get stuck on: the floor plan, the parking plan, and the neighbor letter.

November 28, 2026 The law takes effect. From that date, operating without a license is a violation.
$150 The city's license fee — paid to the city, separate from this packet.
12 items Required in the license application (Section 686B.03(b)).
$500,000 Minimum liability insurance your application must prove.
Start with two certificates Lead-safe certification and a certificate of occupancy are needed before you can file.
$5,000 fine For operating a short-term rental without a license — plus a first-degree misdemeanor.
$1,000 per day For advertising an unlicensed rental. The fine repeats every day the ad stays up.
10% cap Rentals per block or building are limited — if your block fills up first, there may be no room left.

One honest note: as of early November 2026, the City of Cleveland has not yet published the official application form. The ordinance says applications are made on forms supplied by the Commissioner of Assessments and Licenses. This packet cannot file for you — it gets you fully ready, with every document in hand, so you can file the day the form opens instead of starting a multi-week process while the law is already in effect.

Questions

Who exactly needs this license?

Anyone who operates, rents, leases, or subleases a dwelling unit in Cleveland as a short-term rental — a temporary rental for compensation, through a booking agent or directly, for not more than 30 consecutive days (Sections 686B.03(a), 686B.02(j)).

Do I have to live in the property?

No. The law contains no owner-occupancy requirement. You must have a local contact who can be at the property within an hour while guests are there (Section 686B.02(g)), but you do not have to live in the unit yourself.

I'm a tenant. Can I get licensed?

Yes, if your lease allows it. The ordinance counts a tenant with written lease authorization as an "owner" (Section 686B.02(i)), and the application requires proof of a valid lease that allows subleasing (Section 686B.03(b)(8)). A lease that prohibits subleasing is grounds for denial — get written authorization from your landlord first.

I've been hosting for years. Am I grandfathered?

Only in one narrow way, and from the density cap only. A unit that operated as limited lodging under Section 337.251 for at least a year before November 28, 2026, stayed in full compliance, and has no recorded complaints is exempt from the density cap (Section 686B.07(d)). It is not exempt from the license — you still file the full application, pay the fee, and supply all twelve items.

The city hasn't published the application form yet. What do I do now?

Everything in Sections 2, 3, and 5 of the packet. The lead-safe certification, certificate of occupancy, insurance, tax status, and the two plans are all obtainable now, and none of them depend on the city's form. When the forms are published, a prepared host files immediately.

My application was denied. What now?

You may appeal to the Board of Zoning Appeals in writing within 10 days of the denial (Section 686B.04(b)). Act immediately — the window is short — and consider a Cleveland attorney for the appeal.
